# Portfolio & Personal Website Design Standards

## 1. Purpose

Create portfolio and personal websites that communicate a person's value
clearly, establish credibility, showcase their work, and make it easy
for visitors to take the next step.

The result must feel intentional, distinctive, trustworthy, fast,
accessible, and easy to maintain---not like a generic template or an
unedited AI-generated page.

Apply this skill when: - Creating a new portfolio or personal website. -
Redesigning or improving an existing personal website. - Building pages
or components for a portfolio. - Reviewing visual quality, usability,
responsiveness, or accessibility. - Translating a design reference into
a working website.

This skill is technology-independent. Follow the project's existing
framework, language, component system, and conventions. Do not introduce
a new framework or dependency without a clear reason.

## 2. Core principles

Prioritize these principles in order:

1.  **Clarity:** Visitors should understand who the person is, what they
    do, and who they help within a few seconds.
2.  **Credibility:** Claims, experience, projects, outcomes, and
    testimonials must be accurate and supportable.
3.  **Distinctiveness:** The design should express the person's
    professional identity rather than resemble a default template.
4.  **Usability:** Navigation and calls to action should be obvious and
    predictable.
5.  **Content first:** Layout and visual effects must support the
    message, not compete with it.
6.  **Consistency:** Reuse a coherent design system across all pages and
    components.
7.  **Responsive quality:** Every important experience must work well on
    small and large screens.
8.  **Accessibility:** Build for keyboard, assistive technology,
    readable contrast, and different user needs.
9.  **Performance:** Keep pages fast and avoid unnecessary assets,
    scripts, and dependencies.
10. **Maintainability:** Prefer understandable, reusable code over
    clever or overengineered abstractions.

## 3. Understand the person and the audience first

Before implementation, inspect the available project context and
identify:

-   The person's name and preferred public title.
-   Their profession, specialization, and strongest skills.
-   Their target audience: recruiters, hiring managers, clients,
    collaborators, or a community.
-   Their primary website goal: get hired, attract clients, demonstrate
    expertise, publish ideas, or build a personal brand.
-   Their strongest evidence: selected projects, measurable outcomes,
    experience, credentials, writing, talks, or testimonials.
-   The desired tone: for example, technical, approachable, creative,
    executive, or minimal.

Do not invent biography details, employers, client names, project
results, certifications, testimonials, contact information, or social
profiles.

If information is missing: - Use only facts supported by supplied
content or the existing project. - Keep the content structure ready for
the missing information. - Use clearly marked placeholders only when
necessary, such as `[Add project outcome]`. - Do not publish fake
metrics, fake testimonials, or fabricated logos. - Ask a concise
clarification only when the missing information blocks a meaningful
decision.

## 4. Information architecture

Keep the site focused. A portfolio does not need many pages to feel
complete.

Choose the smallest useful structure for the person's goals. Common
sections include:

### Home / Hero

-   Name and a clear professional headline.
-   A short value proposition explaining what the person does.
-   One primary action, such as "View my work" or "Contact me".
-   A secondary action when useful, such as "Download résumé".
-   A relevant portrait, illustration, or restrained visual element when
    available.
-   Avoid vague headlines such as "Welcome to my website" or unsupported
    claims such as "The world's best developer".

### About

-   A concise professional introduction.
-   Relevant background, approach, interests, and working style.
-   Explain what makes the person's experience relevant to the intended
    audience.
-   Avoid long autobiographies and generic motivational copy.

### Selected work / Projects

-   Feature a small, curated set of strong projects rather than an
    unfiltered list.
-   For each project, show its name, context, the person's contribution,
    technologies or methods where relevant, and the result.
-   Link to a live project, case study, repository, or demo only when
    the link is real and available.
-   Clearly distinguish personal, academic, open-source, and
    professional work.
-   Never imply sole ownership of collaborative work. Describe the
    person's contribution accurately.

### Experience

-   Present roles, organizations, dates, and responsibilities
    consistently.
-   Emphasize relevant contributions and outcomes, not only job
    descriptions.
-   Use concise, scannable entries.

### Skills

-   Group skills meaningfully (for example, engineering, design, tools,
    and collaboration).
-   Do not use arbitrary percentage bars or skill ratings without a
    meaningful measurement method.
-   Avoid listing every technology ever encountered.

### Testimonials / Recognition

-   Include only authentic, approved testimonials and verifiable
    recognition.
-   Preserve the meaning and attribution of quotations.
-   Omit the section when there is no credible content.

### Writing / Publications

-   Include articles, talks, tutorials, or other public work when they
    strengthen the person's positioning.
-   Show dates and clear titles.
-   Avoid empty or fabricated publication cards.

### Contact

-   State the preferred contact method clearly.
-   Use working links and validate their destinations.
-   Do not expose private contact details unless explicitly intended for
    publication.
-   Make the next step clear and low-friction.

Do not add every possible section automatically. Select sections based
on the person's goals and the quality of available content.

## 5. Visual direction and design quality

### Establish a visual concept

Before styling, define a coherent direction: - Overall character:
minimal, editorial, technical, expressive, elegant, playful, or another
deliberate direction. - Color palette and contrast roles. - Typography
pairing and type scale. - Spacing rhythm and content width. - Image and
illustration treatment. - Shape language, borders, shadows, and
interaction style.

The design should fit the person's field and personality. Do not assume
every developer needs a dark terminal-inspired website or every creative
professional needs a colorful layout.

### Avoid generic AI design

Do not default to: - A predictable gradient hero with oversized text and
floating decorative blobs. - Excessive glassmorphism, neon glow, or
blurred cards. - Random gradients, icons, and decorative shapes without
purpose. - The same three-card grid repeated for every section. - Too
many pills, badges, borders, and rounded containers. - Unnecessary
animations and scroll effects. - Huge empty areas that make the page
feel unfinished. - Stock imagery that does not relate to the person or
their work. - Emoji used as a substitute for a considered icon system. -
Fake terminal windows, code snippets, charts, or metrics used only as
decoration.

Use visual effects only when they strengthen hierarchy, identity, or
comprehension.

### Layout and hierarchy

-   Create a clear reading order and visual hierarchy.
-   Use a consistent content container and alignment system.
-   Keep line lengths comfortable for reading.
-   Use whitespace to separate ideas and establish rhythm.
-   Avoid both cramped layouts and excessive empty space.
-   Vary section composition where it improves storytelling, while
    preserving consistency.
-   Ensure the first screen communicates identity, value, and a useful
    next action.
-   Make project previews visually informative, not merely decorative
    boxes.

### Typography

-   Use a deliberate type scale with clear roles for display, heading,
    body, labels, and metadata.
-   Prefer readable typefaces and appropriate weights.
-   Keep body text comfortably readable on mobile and desktop.
-   Use line height and paragraph spacing that support scanning.
-   Avoid too many font families, weights, uppercase labels, or
    excessively small text.
-   Do not use low-contrast muted text for essential information.

### Color

-   Define semantic color roles: background, surface, text, muted text,
    border, primary action, and focus.
-   Maintain sufficient contrast for text and interactive controls.
-   Use accent colors purposefully and consistently.
-   Do not rely on color alone to communicate status or meaning.
-   Check the entire page in every supported theme, if light/dark themes
    are provided.

### Images and media

-   Prefer authentic portraits, project screenshots, product images, and
    diagrams supplied by the user.
-   Use images that provide context or evidence.
-   Crop consistently and preserve important subject matter.
-   Set image dimensions or aspect ratios to reduce layout shifts.
-   Provide meaningful alternative text for informative images; use
    empty alt text for purely decorative images.
-   Optimize image size and format.
-   Do not hotlink arbitrary images or use assets without permission.
-   Do not create fake screenshots or imply a mockup is a live product.

## 6. Responsive and mobile-first behavior

Design and verify the experience across narrow phones, larger phones,
tablets, laptops, and wide desktops.

-   Start with a usable narrow-screen layout, then enhance it for wider
    screens.
-   Use fluid sizing and sensible maximum widths.
-   Avoid fixed widths that cause horizontal scrolling.
-   Ensure navigation is usable by touch and keyboard.
-   Stack multi-column layouts when space is insufficient.
-   Keep headings, buttons, forms, and cards from overflowing.
-   Make tap targets comfortably large and separated.
-   Ensure project screenshots and media scale without distortion.
-   Do not simply shrink the desktop layout; reconsider hierarchy and
    spacing for mobile.
-   Test long names, long project titles, translated text if applicable,
    and unusually narrow viewports.

No important content or action may disappear on mobile without a
deliberate, justified reason.

## 7. Accessibility

Aim for WCAG 2.2 AA as the practical target.

-   Use semantic landmarks and meaningful heading order.
-   Use native HTML elements for links, buttons, forms, and controls.
-   Ensure all interactive functionality works with a keyboard.
-   Provide a visible focus indicator.
-   Give form fields persistent, programmatic labels and useful error
    messages.
-   Provide accessible names for icon-only controls.
-   Use sufficient text and UI contrast.
-   Do not use color as the only indicator.
-   Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
-   Avoid flashing content and unnecessary motion.
-   Use descriptive link text; avoid repeated ambiguous labels such as
    "Click here".
-   Ensure dialogs, menus, and navigation patterns support keyboard and
    assistive technology.
-   Do not use ARIA when native HTML already provides the required
    semantics.

## 8. Interaction and motion

Interactions should be clear, responsive, and purposeful.

-   Provide hover, focus, active, and disabled states where relevant.
-   Use consistent transitions and keep them subtle.
-   Make clickable areas match their visible affordance.
-   Ensure buttons and links perform the action their labels promise.
-   Provide feedback for form submission, validation, loading, and
    errors.
-   Avoid interactions that depend only on hover.
-   Avoid scroll hijacking, forced animations, and motion that delays
    access to content.
-   Keep essential content available if animation or JavaScript is
    unavailable.
-   Use motion to clarify state changes or guide attention---not as
    decoration everywhere.

## 9. Content and writing standards

Write for the intended visitor, not to impress other designers.

-   Use specific, plain, confident language.
-   Prefer concrete contributions and evidence over buzzwords.
-   Keep headings descriptive and concise.
-   Make paragraphs easy to scan.
-   Use consistent tense, capitalization, punctuation, and terminology.
-   Explain technical terms when the target audience may not know them.
-   Avoid exaggerated claims and generic phrases such as "passionate
    rockstar" or "pixel-perfect ninja".
-   Preserve the person's voice and meaning when editing supplied copy.
-   Do not silently change factual claims.

For project case studies, use a useful structure: 1. Context: What was
the project and why did it matter? 2. Challenge: What problem needed to
be solved? 3. Role: What did the person own or contribute? 4. Approach:
What decisions and work were involved? 5. Outcome: What changed, with
evidence where available? 6. Reflection: What was learned, if relevant?

## 10. Navigation and conversion

-   Keep primary navigation short and predictable.
-   Use clear labels that match section names.
-   Make the primary call to action easy to find without making every
    section a sales pitch.
-   Provide a consistent way to return to the home page.
-   Use real destinations for résumé, email, social, repository, and
    project links.
-   Indicate external destinations when useful.
-   Avoid dead links, placeholder `#` links, and buttons with no
    behavior.
-   Do not make contact forms collect more information than necessary.
-   Provide a clear success and failure state for forms.
-   Never claim a message was sent unless the implementation confirms
    it.

## 11. SEO, sharing, and discoverability

-   Give every page a unique, descriptive title and meta description.
-   Use a clear heading structure and meaningful page content.
-   Set canonical URLs when the deployment setup supports them.
-   Configure Open Graph and social sharing metadata.
-   Use a suitable favicon and social preview image when assets are
    available.
-   Use descriptive URLs and link text.
-   Add structured data only when accurate and appropriate.
-   Provide a sitemap and robots directives when relevant to the
    deployment.
-   Avoid keyword stuffing and duplicate metadata.
-   Ensure important content is rendered and discoverable by search
    engines.
-   Do not invent public URLs, social handles, or structured-data facts.

## 12. Performance and technical quality

-   Keep dependencies minimal and justify each added dependency.
-   Optimize and appropriately size images.
-   Lazy-load below-the-fold media when appropriate; do not lazy-load
    the primary hero image if it harms perceived loading.
-   Avoid unnecessary client-side JavaScript.
-   Prevent layout shifts by reserving media dimensions.
-   Use efficient fonts and limit font variants.
-   Avoid large animation libraries for simple effects.
-   Keep browser console errors and failed network requests at zero.
-   Handle loading, empty, success, and error states.
-   Use secure link attributes when opening external links in a new tab.
-   Do not expose secrets, private API keys, or personal data in
    client-side code.
-   Follow the repository's linting, formatting, typing, and testing
    conventions.
-   Avoid broad refactors unrelated to the requested website work.

Use Core Web Vitals as quality signals. Aim for good field performance
where measurable: LCP at or below 2.5 seconds, INP at or below 200
milliseconds, and CLS at or below 0.1 at the 75th percentile. Do not
claim these targets are met without measurement.

## 13. Implementation standards

-   Inspect the existing repository before changing files.
-   Follow the current architecture, naming conventions, routing, and
    styling approach.
-   Reuse existing components and tokens when they are suitable.
-   Create reusable components when they represent a real repeated
    pattern---not merely to split every small element.
-   Keep content separate from presentation when it improves
    maintainability.
-   Use design tokens or centralized variables for colors, spacing,
    typography, radii, and shadows.
-   Keep components focused and avoid unnecessary prop complexity.
-   Use semantic HTML and native browser capabilities.
-   Keep state and interactions as simple as possible.
-   Avoid duplicate CSS, conflicting overrides, magic numbers, and
    brittle selectors.
-   Do not add placeholder content that could accidentally ship.
-   Do not modify unrelated files or remove existing functionality
    without a reason.
-   Follow the project's accessibility and security practices.

## 14. Required workflow

### Step 1 --- Inspect

Review the repository, current pages, framework, dependencies, assets,
routing, styling system, and existing design conventions. Identify what
already works and what must be preserved.

### Step 2 --- Define

Summarize the target audience, primary goal, content available, page
structure, visual direction, and key interactions. If the task is small
and context is clear, do this briefly without creating unnecessary
documentation.

### Step 3 --- Plan

Create a concise implementation plan. Identify reusable components,
responsive behavior, required assets, and any missing content that
blocks completion.

### Step 4 --- Build

Implement the page using the project's conventions. Establish design
tokens, build semantic structure, add content, and implement
interactions. Prioritize a complete and coherent experience over
decorative complexity.

### Step 5 --- Verify

Run the available build, lint, type-check, and test commands where
practical. Inspect the rendered website at mobile, tablet, and desktop
widths. Check links, forms, navigation, keyboard behavior, contrast, and
console errors.

If browser preview or automated visual testing is unavailable, state
that limitation honestly. Do not claim to have visually tested a page
that was not rendered or inspected.

### Step 6 --- Refine

Fix visual inconsistencies, overflow, awkward spacing, weak hierarchy,
inaccessible controls, broken states, and unnecessary complexity.
Recheck after changes.

### Step 7 --- Report

Summarize: - What was created or changed. - Important design and
implementation decisions. - Checks actually performed and their
results. - Any remaining content, assets, or verification needed.

Do not claim a check passed if it was not run.

## 15. Definition of done

A portfolio or personal website is ready only when the applicable items
below are satisfied.

### Purpose and content

-   [ ] The visitor can understand who the person is and what they do
    quickly.
-   [ ] The primary audience and next action are clear.
-   [ ] All factual content is supported by supplied information.
-   [ ] Projects accurately describe the person's contribution.
-   [ ] No fake metrics, testimonials, credentials, or broken
    placeholder content remain.

### Visual design

-   [ ] The design has a deliberate visual direction.
-   [ ] Typography, spacing, colors, and components are consistent.
-   [ ] The page has a clear hierarchy and readable content width.
-   [ ] Visual effects support the content rather than distract from it.
-   [ ] Images are relevant, optimized, and displayed correctly.

### Responsive behavior

-   [ ] The page works at phone, tablet, laptop, and wide desktop
    widths.
-   [ ] There is no unintended horizontal overflow.
-   [ ] Navigation, media, forms, and calls to action work on touch
    devices.
-   [ ] Content remains readable and complete at narrow widths.

### Accessibility

-   [ ] Semantic structure and heading order are appropriate.
-   [ ] Keyboard navigation and visible focus work.
-   [ ] Controls have accessible names and labels.
-   [ ] Contrast is sufficient.
-   [ ] Motion preferences are respected.

### Functionality and quality

-   [ ] All links and buttons have correct behavior.
-   [ ] Forms provide validation and feedback.
-   [ ] Loading, empty, and error states are handled where applicable.
-   [ ] No known console errors or broken assets remain.
-   [ ] Build, lint, type checks, and relevant tests pass---or failures
    are reported.
-   [ ] Performance and metadata have been reviewed.
-   [ ] No unrelated functionality was broken.

## 16. Decision rules

When trade-offs arise:

-   Prefer clarity over visual novelty.
-   Prefer authentic content over decorative filler.
-   Prefer a few strong projects over a large weak collection.
-   Prefer accessible native controls over custom interactions.
-   Prefer responsive simplicity over complex layout tricks.
-   Prefer measured performance over unsupported performance claims.
-   Prefer the existing project conventions over unnecessary rewrites.
-   Prefer asking for a missing fact over inventing it.
-   Prefer a complete, polished, modest website over an ambitious but
    unfinished one.

## 17. Final instruction to the agent

Treat this skill as the default quality bar for portfolio and personal
website work. Adapt the visual direction to the individual and the task;
do not apply one fixed template to every person. Make deliberate design
decisions, preserve factual accuracy, verify the real implementation,
and report limitations honestly.
